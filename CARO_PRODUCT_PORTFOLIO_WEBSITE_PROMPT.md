# MASTER PROMPT - Caroline Product Portfolio Website

## Mission

Design and build a premium one-page personal portfolio website for Caroline, intended to accompany job applications and reposition her professional narrative from primarily project/delivery execution toward product thinking.

The site must feel like a deliberately designed product, not a CV copied into a webpage. Its job is to make a recruiter or hiring manager understand within seconds:

1. who Caroline is,
2. how she thinks,
3. what kind of problems she can solve,
4. what evidence supports that positioning,
5. why they should remember and contact her.

The portfolio should communicate product mindset through the information architecture, interaction design, case-study structure, visual hierarchy and writing itself.

## Important source-of-truth rule

Use the supplied CV as the factual source of truth for Caroline's actual background. Do not invent product roles, product launches, metrics, employers, achievements, responsibilities or artefacts that are not documented.

The current CV describes a bilingual French/English Talent Acquisition and Recruitment Coordination professional with 4+ years of experience across recruitment, HR operations and financial services. Her experience includes Lawrence Harvey, Emerique & Partners and Ecolab, with strengths around stakeholder management, candidate experience, market intelligence, workforce planning, organisational change and end-to-end processes.

The website is intended to support a career transition. Therefore, translate transferable experience into product-relevant capabilities without falsely rewriting her employment history.

## Strategic positioning

The central narrative should be:

> Caroline is moving from delivery-focused work toward product-oriented problem solving, bringing strong experience in understanding users and stakeholders, identifying needs, coordinating complex workflows, using market insight, improving experiences and making decisions across competing constraints.

Do not simply label her a Product Manager unless she has explicitly approved that title.

Use language such as:

- Product-minded operator
- Product thinking
- User and stakeholder insight
- Problem framing
- Discovery
- Prioritisation
- Experience design
- Evidence-led decisions
- Cross-functional execution
- Systems thinking

Treat these as positioning concepts, not fabricated employment claims.

## Inspiration analysis

### Scotty Peterson

Reference: https://www.scottypeterson.net/

Borrow the principles, not the visual identity.

Useful characteristics:

- Extremely clear hero proposition.
- A memorable one-sentence statement explains what he does immediately.
- Strong editorial hierarchy with numbered sections.
- The portfolio explains a professional point of view rather than merely listing jobs.
- Experience is supported by selected builds and evidence.
- Long-form writing is treated as proof of expertise.
- Simple navigation and direct calls to action.
- The site creates a narrative from About -> Experience -> Work -> Skills -> Contact.

For Caroline, reproduce this clarity but avoid making the design feel like a developer or engineering portfolio.

### Andrey Mitko

Reference: https://www.andreymitko.com/

Borrow:

- Elegant and restrained portfolio structure.
- Strong opening professional statement.
- Clear About and Experience sections.
- Experience cards containing company context, role, location and industry.
- Selected projects presented as visual editorial objects.
- Spacious layout and high-quality typography.
- Simple final contact area.

Do not copy the black/white visual treatment. Caroline wants more personality and warmth.

## Creative direction

Create a distinctive editorial/product-design identity that sits between:

- modern product portfolio,
- premium editorial magazine,
- sophisticated personal brand,
- subtle digital experimentation.

Avoid:

- generic SaaS gradients,
- template-looking portfolio cards,
- excessive glassmorphism,
- giant meaningless animations,
- startup cliché illustrations,
- skill percentage bars,
- a conventional CV timeline as the dominant experience,
- overuse of black and white,
- corporate HR visual language.

### Visual personality

Aim for: intelligent, curious, warm, structured, confident, contemporary.

Use an off-white or lightly tinted canvas rather than pure white. Introduce one distinctive signature accent colour plus a restrained supporting palette. The exact palette should be explored during design rather than hard-coded prematurely.

Typography should carry much of the personality. Consider a sophisticated editorial display face paired with a highly legible modern sans-serif. Use large typographic moments, but preserve readability.

Use a consistent visual motif derived from product thinking, for example connected observations, signals, sticky-note fragments, decision paths, highlighted evidence, annotations, or a subtle cursor/selection language. Choose one system and use it consistently.

## One-page information architecture

### 00. Persistent navigation

Compact navigation with anchors:

- Home
- About
- Experience
- Artefacts
- Contact

Include a small but visible CTA such as `Let's talk` or `LinkedIn`.

On mobile, keep navigation extremely simple.

### 01. Hero / Overview

This is the most important section.

Do not open with `Hi, I'm Caroline` as the primary message.

Create a sharp positioning sentence in the spirit of Scotty Peterson's clarity.

Develop 3 candidate headline directions before selecting the strongest. Examples of direction only:

- `I turn messy human problems into clearer product decisions.`
- `From understanding people to shaping better products.`
- `I connect user needs, business context and execution.`

These are starting points, not mandatory final copy.

Below the headline, add a concise paragraph explaining the transition and the value Caroline brings.

Include two CTAs:

- Explore my thinking
- About my experience

Optionally add a small status label such as `London · Exploring Product opportunities`, but only if Caroline confirms the exact role targets.

The hero should have one signature interactive or visual device. It should communicate product thinking rather than exist as decoration.

Possible concept: a small interactive `How I think` flow with 4 states:

Observe -> Frame -> Explore -> Decide

As the cursor moves or the user scrolls, each state reveals a short sentence.

### 02. Product mindset / How I think

Before the chronological CV, establish why Caroline's previous experience is relevant.

Create 3 to 4 concise principles such as:

1. Start with people
2. Frame the real problem
3. Use evidence, not assumptions
4. Make complexity actionable

Each principle should eventually be supported by a real example or artefact.

This section should visually behave more like a product framework than a list of soft skills.

### 03. About

Tell a short narrative about the transition.

Suggested structure:

- Where I come from
- What I learned from working close to candidates, employees, hiring managers and organisations
- What increasingly interested me beyond delivery
- Why product is the logical next direction
- What I am currently exploring/building/writing

Keep this human and concise.

Do not disparage recruitment, HR or project/delivery work. Present the transition as an expansion of existing strengths.

For each role show:

### 05. Artefacts / Product thinking

This is strategically critical because Caroline is transitioning domains.

The section should be positioned as evidence of thinking, not `My Blog`.

Preferred title directions:

- Selected thinking
- Product artefacts
- Notes from the work
- How I approach problems

Create a modular artefact system that can support several content types:

- Product teardown
- Case study
- Discovery exercise
- User journey
- Problem framing
- Prioritisation exercise
- Experiment proposal
- Retrospective
- Opinion / essay

Every artefact card should display:

- type,
- title,
- one-line question/problem,
- short summary,
- date,
- estimated read time,
- optional methods/tags,
- visual thumbnail or generated diagram.

Opening an artefact may use a dedicated route/page even though the primary portfolio is one-page. If strict one-page architecture is required, use an accessible modal or expanded reading view with deep-link support.

### Artefact case-study template

Every substantial case study should use a repeatable product narrative:

1. Context
2. Problem
3. What I observed
4. Assumptions
5. Evidence / research
6. User or stakeholder needs
7. Options considered
8. Prioritisation / trade-offs
9. Proposed solution
10. How I would measure success
11. What I learned / what I would test next

This structure is intentionally more valuable than merely showing polished screens. Recruiters should be able to inspect Caroline's reasoning.

### 06. Experience-to-Product bridge

Add a small visual section translating prior experience into product capabilities.

Example mapping:

`Candidate interviews -> User discovery`

`Hiring-manager requirements -> Stakeholder discovery / problem framing`

`Market mapping -> Competitive research`

`Candidate experience -> Journey / experience thinking`

`Multiple vacancies -> Prioritisation under constraints`

`Feedback cycles -> Iterative decision-making`

`Workforce planning -> Business context and strategic thinking`

Make clear that these are transferable parallels, not claims that the activities are identical.

### 07. Contact

Finish with a strong, minimal statement rather than a generic contact form.

Example direction:

`Have a product problem worth thinking about? Let's talk.`

Include:

- LinkedIn
- Email
- optional downloadable CV

Use the contact details supplied by Caroline in the implementation data layer rather than hard-coding them throughout the UI.

## Interaction design

Motion should reinforce hierarchy and thinking.

Use:

- restrained scroll reveals,
- smooth anchor transitions,
- elegant hover states,
- subtle typographic transitions,
- animated connectors or annotations in the product-thinking framework,
- lightweight artefact-card interactions,
- reduced-motion support.

Avoid scroll hijacking.

The website must remain excellent without animation.

## Responsive design

Design desktop and mobile intentionally rather than simply stacking desktop blocks.

On mobile:

- simplify decorative elements,
- maintain large readable typography,
- preserve the hero proposition above the fold where practical,
- turn experience into compact expandable entries if useful,
- keep artefact cards highly scannable,
- ensure tap targets and focus states are accessible.

## UX and accessibility requirements

- Semantic HTML.
- WCAG-conscious colour contrast.
- Full keyboard navigation.
- Visible focus states.
- Reduced-motion preference.
- Alt text for meaningful imagery.
- No critical information hidden exclusively behind hover.
- Fast initial load.
- Excellent Lighthouse targets.

## Technical recommendation

Unless the existing repository imposes another stack, prefer:

- Next.js
- TypeScript
- Tailwind CSS
- Framer Motion only where animation adds value
- MDX or a lightweight content collection for artefacts

The artefact system must make publishing a new article easy without editing layout code.

Suggested content model:

```ts
interface Artifact {
  slug: string
  title: string
  type: 'case-study' | 'teardown' | 'essay' | 'discovery' | 'experiment'
  summary: string
  question?: string
  publishedAt: string
  readingTime?: string
  tags: string[]
  featured?: boolean
  cover?: string
}
```

## Content architecture

Separate content from presentation.

Create structured data for:

- profile,
- navigation,
- principles,
- experience,
- education,
- artefacts,
- social/contact links.

This should allow Caroline to update content without touching component internals.

## Signature design requirement

Before implementation, generate 3 genuinely different visual concepts.

For each concept provide:

- concept name,
- design thesis,
- palette direction,
- typography direction,
- hero treatment,
- signature motif,
- artefact-card treatment,
- motion language,
- why it supports Caroline's positioning.

Then score each concept against:

- memorability,
- professionalism,
- product credibility,
- readability,
- differentiation,
- maintainability.

Choose the strongest concept and implement it consistently.

Do not combine random elements from all three concepts unless there is a clear design-system rationale.

## Suggested concept territory

One promising direction to explore is an `Editorial Product Notebook`.

Imagine a premium editorial portfolio where Caroline's observations, decisions and artefacts appear as annotated evidence. The interface can use small marginal notes, index numbers, highlighted phrases and diagram-like connectors. It should feel designed by someone who organises ambiguity into clarity.

This is a direction, not a restriction. Explore alternatives before selecting it.

## Copywriting rules

The copy must be:

- concise,
- intelligent,
- specific,
- conversational but professional,
- free from generic product jargon.

Avoid unsupported claims such as:

- `I build products users love`
- `Experienced Product Manager`
- `Proven product leader`

unless evidence is supplied later.

Prefer showing reasoning through artefacts.

## Recruiter scan test

A recruiter spending only 20 seconds on the page should leave knowing:

- Caroline is intentionally moving toward Product.
- She has substantial professional experience rather than being an entry-level graduate.
- Her previous work gives her strong user/stakeholder, research, coordination and decision-making foundations.
- She can demonstrate product thinking through concrete artefacts.
- She is based in London and can be contacted easily.

## Build phases

### Phase 1 - Discovery

1. Inspect all supplied CV and artefact material.
2. Extract verified facts.
3. Identify missing information.
4. Do not invent missing metrics or projects.
5. Produce a content inventory.
6. Produce the 3 design directions.

### Phase 2 - Information architecture and wireframe

Create desktop and mobile wireframes for the complete journey.

Validate:

- hero clarity,
- story order,
- transition narrative,
- experience hierarchy,
- artefact discoverability,
- CTA visibility.

### Phase 3 - Visual system

Define:

- colour tokens,
- typography scale,
- spacing system,
- grid,
- borders/radii,
- iconography,
- image treatment,
- motion tokens,
- card system,
- annotation system.

### Phase 4 - Implementation

Build reusable production-quality components.

Do not create a static screenshot disguised as a website.

### Phase 5 - Content integration

Populate only verified CV information and supplied artefacts.

Where copy or product evidence is missing, use clearly marked content placeholders such as:

`[CONTENT NEEDED: first product teardown]`

Never fabricate evidence to make the portfolio look complete.

### Phase 6 - QA

Test:

- desktop,
- laptop,
- tablet,
- mobile,
- keyboard navigation,
- reduced motion,
- responsive typography,
- broken links,
- layout overflow,
- loading performance,
- accessibility,
- metadata and social previews.

### Phase 7 - Recruiter critique

Review the finished site from three perspectives:

1. Product recruiter
2. Hiring manager
3. Senior Product Manager

For each, answer:

- What do I understand in 10 seconds?
- What feels credible?
- What feels unsupported?
- What would make me continue reading?
- What is missing before I would interview Caroline?

Then make the highest-value corrections.

The portfolio must not pretend Caroline has already had a Product title.

Its strongest argument should instead be visible evidence:

**Here is how I understand problems, structure ambiguity, consider users and stakeholders, make trade-offs, communicate decisions and learn.**

Make the website itself the first product artefact.
